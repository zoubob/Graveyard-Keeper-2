## What Is Factory Automation in Graveyard Keeper 2?

Factory automation is the sequel's answer to the slowest part of the original. In Graveyard Keeper 1, putting the dead to work meant a zombie chopping wood or carrying stone on the surface — a modest help that arrived late. In Graveyard Keeper 2, community coverage describes complete assembly lines staffed by workers who never tire, running in dedicated underground complexes.

The framing the coverage uses is worth keeping, because it explains the design intent: necromantic capitalism. The undead stop being a threat you manage and become a workforce you exploit, and every automation decision maps directly onto how much profit the crisis produces. It is dark humor functioning as a game system rather than as dialogue.

That reframing also tells you what the system is for. Automation is not a convenience feature bolted onto the economy; it is the economy's growth engine. For the fundamentals of raising the workers themselves, start with the [main zombie guide](/zombies/zombie-guide/); for the routing layer that keeps them supplied, the [logistics network guide](/zombies/logistics-network/) is the prerequisite reading.

## What Does an Underground Factory Look Like?

Community coverage sketches the layout in three moves:

1. **The base descends.** Heavy manufacturing moves below ground rather than spreading across the surface.
2. **Excavation fronts run themselves.** Instead of you draining your energy bar to mine and haul stone, the excavation runs day and night without you.
3. **Extraction centralizes.** Concentrating heavy extraction underground leaves the surface free for the constructions and rituals that generate income.

The result described is a set of dark rooms functioning as sheds, with the machinery operated directly by the workforce and output flowing continuously. The named products — ingots, planks, and alchemical reagents — are the same intermediate goods your manual production was already making, just produced without your involvement.

The strategic logic is worth stating plainly, because it is the part that transfers even without verified numbers: **the surface is your income layer and the underground is your throughput layer.** Rituals, the church, and visitor-facing buildings want surface space; smelting, refining, and heavy processing want a hole in the ground where nothing else is competing for room.

## Why Is the Corpse Quality the Only Real Investment?

This is the most interesting claim in the coverage, and it inverts the first game's instincts.

On the graveyard side, you want clean bodies with white skulls, because those raise your cemetery rating and your sermon income. On the factory side, the reported rule is the opposite: corruption — red skulls — defines the worker's evolution ceiling and unlocks its technical potential on heavy machines. The coverage puts it bluntly: the more rotted the body, the better the employee, and a player chasing factory output actively seeks out the corrupted corpses.

That creates a sorting decision every time a body arrives, and coverage treats it as the routine's central puzzle rather than a nuisance:

| The body | Where it goes | What it feeds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clean, high white skulls | The grave | Cemetery rating and sermon income |
| Corrupted, high red skulls | The resurrection table | The underground workshop |

The consequence is that automation costs you almost nothing in materials but everything in attention. Coverage describes the only real investment as the quality of the body on the autopsy table — no payroll, no upkeep, just the steady discipline of routing each corpse to the side that values it. The [skull system explainer](/zombies/skull-system-explained/) covers what the two skull types mean, and the [embalming guide](/zombies/embalming-and-red-skulls/) covers shaping a body toward the ceiling you want.

## How Does the Game Change Once It Runs?

The reported shift is about where your attention goes. Repetitive tasks leave your hands, and your focus moves to route planning and logistics — designing the flow instead of performing it. Coverage describes running quests, exploration, and commerce while the workshop produces in the background, and credits the system with deleting most of the sequel's early-game slowness.

That is the real payoff, and it is worth understanding as an attention trade rather than a resource one. Automation does not make the game easier; it changes which game you are playing. You stop being labor and start being management, and the problems you solve become layout, sorting, and supply.

The [money-making guide](/guides/money-making-guide/) covers what to do with the output once it exists, the [town rebuilding guide](/guides/town-rebuilding/) covers the surface layer that the underground is funding, and the [performance guide](/guides/performance-settings/) is worth reading before you scale — running dozens of autonomous workers at once is exactly the load that early testing flagged as expensive.

## What Is Still Unverified?

This page describes a system whose shape is well reported and whose numbers are not. Being specific about the gap:

- **Complex size and machine counts.** Coverage mentions underground complexes and assembly lines without quantifying them. How much floor space this consumes, and how many stations it holds, is unconfirmed.
- **Throughput rates.** No output figures per hour or per worker are published anywhere credible. The claim that the workshop runs continuously is directional, not measured.
- **Unlock requirements.** When the underground layer becomes available, and what gates it, is not documented in our sources.
- **Interaction with the debuff on oversized hordes.** Community coverage separately reports that too many zombies carries a penalty, and how that interacts with a genuinely industrial-scale workforce is unresolved. The [zombie overload page](/zombies/overload-debuff/) covers what is known.
- **Advanced-save performance.** Whether a large underground factory degrades frame rate is the open question performance coverage raised and did not answer.

The honest posture is the one this whole site takes: build the shape, measure the scale yourself, and do not trust a throughput number that nobody has published. What is safely true is the design intent — heavy industry goes underground, corruption is the qualification that matters, and the point of the whole system is to buy back your own attention.
